The kestrel over the bypass

By A. Writer, Thursday

The bird has been there every morning this month, holding station over the verge where the slip road meets the dual carriageway, as still as something hung on a wire. Drivers who notice it at all take it for a hawk, which it is, and for a hunter, which it also is, though what it is hunting has more to do with the mowing schedule than with anything the birds were doing here before the road arrived.

Verges are, by accident, some of the better grassland left in the county. They are cut late, they are never grazed, and nobody has ever put fertiliser on one. Voles do well in them, and where voles do well a kestrel will hover, and where a kestrel hovers a great many people will drive past underneath without ever looking up at it.

The county council counts the verges as an asset and mows them twice a year, which is roughly what the voles want, and it does this for reasons of sightlines rather than of voles. That is how most conservation happens: as a side effect of somebody else's budget, arrived at for reasons that have nothing to do with the thing being conserved.

It will be gone by August, when the grass goes over and the voles move down into the thatch where a bird cannot see them. Until then it is there every morning, roughly above the second marker post, doing the one thing that everybody knows kestrels for and almost nobody stops to watch.
